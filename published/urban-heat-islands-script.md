## Slide 4 — Dark surfaces make cities hotter than their surroundings

_Presenter: Asha Rao · definition_

Step off a park lawn onto black asphalt on a summer afternoon and you can feel the city turn up the heat under your feet. That's where I want to start our first section on measuring urban heat, because before we fix anything we have to name why cities run hotter than the countryside around them. Think of every roof, road and parking lot as making a choice with sunlight. Scientists score that choice as albedo, the fraction of sunlight that is diffusely reflected by a body, from zero for a black body that absorbs all incident radiation to one for a body that reflects all incident radiation. Dark, dense materials and lost vegetation tilt us toward zero — they soak up sun and then re-radiate it as heat. The contrast is stark. Fresh asphalt sits around 0.04 while fresh snow sits around 0.80, and on a city scale that adds up: urbanization generally leaves us commonly 0.01–0.02 lower than adjacent croplands, which contributes to global warming. So this simple zero-to-one scale is our thermometer for the built environment. If we can measure reflectivity, we can target it — and that physics is exactly what we'll use next when we talk about cool roofs and pavements.

## Slide 5 — Over-86°F days doubled from 14 to 32 per summer

_Presenter: Asha Rao · chart_

Forget the record heat wave for a second — because the danger isn't only those few scorching afternoons. What we're seeing in New York is that entire summers are shifting hotter, and that's what's killing people. Over five decades, days when that four-day average tops 86 degrees have more than doubled, from an average of 14 to 32 days per summer, and that hottest range is growing faster than any other. It's not an anomaly anymore, it's the new season. And our models catch what death certificates miss. When you compare deaths on hot days to cooler days from May through September for 2014 to 2023, you find an estimated annual average of 489 heat-exacerbated deaths — heat quietly worsening heart disease and other chronic illness. That's approximately 3% of all natural-cause deaths in those warm months, out of about 19,000 natural deaths each warm season. So as we open this first part on measuring urban heat, keep that link in mind: more heat means more death, even outside extreme-heat emergencies. Next, we need to ask who is most at risk, and why home is where that risk concentrates.

## Slide 6 — Dark, bare blocks trap heat that feeds on itself

_Presenter: Asha Rao · diagram_

Stand on fresh blacktop at midday in Hermosillo and you feel it before you understand it. That searing heat under your shoes is physics you can measure. Albedo is simply the fraction of sunlight a surface reflects, on a scale from zero, which absorbs all incident radiation, to one, which reflects it all. Fresh asphalt sits at 0.04, meaning it absorbs almost all sun. Even when it wears and lightens, worn asphalt only climbs to 0.12, so dark streets stay hot. Compare that to green grass at 0.25, which reflects far more sun and cools the air around it. Now strip that grass away. In the northwest portion of the city, vegetation cover is just 0% to 6%, lost to concrete and bare ground, and that zone can feel up to 14 degrees C hotter than cooler areas of the city. Dark absorbs, heat builds, vegetation disappears, heat builds more — it reinforces itself. That is why we start this first part, Measuring Urban Heat, here, not with citywide averages but with blocks. If we can map where low reflectivity and bare ground overlap, we know exactly where interventions have to land, which is what we will turn to next when we talk about cooling streets and surfaces.

## Slide 8 — Cool roofs cut heat most where redlining left it hottest

_Presenter: Kabir Mehta · chart_

In Atlanta, your address can decide how breathable your air is on a summer afternoon. We've spent the first part of this story learning how to measure that gap, now we're asking what actually closes it. On the Westside, predominantly Black neighborhoods run 5.6 to 8.4 degrees C hotter than Atlanta's most forested areas. That's not an accident. In the 1930s those blocks were marked hazardous and redlined, starved of investment, left with the lowest tree canopy and the most asphalt and concrete. And while 96% of residents elsewhere in the city have air conditioning at home, less than 80% on the Westside do. That's why the modeling we did with Georgia Tech and the Smart Surfaces Coalition matters. Read left to right: reflective roofs cool citywide air by around 1.4 degrees C, but in those historically redlined neighborhoods the cut reaches as much as 3.5 degrees C. The biggest relief lands exactly where the heat was made worst. And Atlanta acted on it. In June 2025 the council unanimously required all new and replacement roofs to be cool starting in 2026, a move projected at more than $300 million in energy savings over 35 years. So if roofs can do this, what about our streets?

## Slide 9 — Same Street, Two Futures: Trap or Reflect

_Presenter: Kabir Mehta · before-after_

Walk down any blacktopped block in late July and you feel it before you measure it — the heat rising off the road, the glare, nowhere to hide. That's where we ended up in the last section: we can map heat. Now we have to remake it. Picture the street on the left. Fresh asphalt with an albedo of 0.04. That means it reflects just 4% of the sun and absorbs 96%. Add bare metal, no trees, no shade, and every square metre becomes a storage heater that keeps radiating long after sunset. Now picture the same street rebuilt. New concrete with an albedo of 0.55, cool roofs overhead, street trees throwing real shade, water where pavement used to be. You haven't moved the street, you've flipped its physics from trap to reflect. That's the shift for this whole second act. We're done diagnosing hot spots — we're redesigning surfaces so they send sunlight back instead of soaking it in. If we get this right block by block, the corridor stops being an oven and starts behaving like an oasis. Let me show you how each of those pieces pays off.

## Slide 10 — No single surface cools the street alone

_Presenter: Kabir Mehta · layered-architecture_

If you stood barefoot on fresh asphalt at noon, you'd feel why we can't fix heat with one trick. We've mapped where the city runs hottest, and now we turn to how we cool those streets layer by layer, because no single surface does it alone. Think of it from the sun down. First, tree canopy intercepts sunlight before it ever hits the ground, and here choice matters — deciduous trees sit at about 0.15 to 0.18, reflecting more than conifers, while giving us shade that asphalt at 0.04 can never give. Next, cool roofs reflect that heat back upward. Our modeling in Atlanta showed upgrading roofs could lower citywide air temperatures by around 1.4 degrees C (2.4 degrees F), and by as much as 3.5 degrees C (6.3 degrees F) in historically redlined neighborhoods where heat concentrates. But that gain leaks away if the ground still radiates, so we need a cooler, high-albedo, permeable pavement to keep the ground plane from re-heating the air you walk through. And finally the base — moist soil and stormwater held in place — evaporates stored heat instead of banking it overnight. Shade first, reflect second, cool the ground, then let water finish the job. Get that stack right and the whole street holds its cool.

## Slide 11 — Cool roofs win cheap and fast; trees win big and slow

_Presenter: Kabir Mehta · matrix_

So we've mapped where heat hits hardest. Now the question is, what do we actually build? Not every fix works everywhere, and that's the trap. Think about it as a trade-off between cost and cooling plus equity. On one end, you have cool roofs — cheap, fast, scalable. In Atlanta, modeling showed upgrading to reflective roofs could lower citywide air temperatures by around 1.4 degrees C (2.4 degrees F), and by as much as 3.5 degrees C (6.3 degrees F) in historically redlined neighborhoods, with more than $300 million in total energy savings over the next 35 years. In Cape Town's Khayelitsha, they're rolling reflective roof paint across homes for the same reason: studies show cool roofs can lower indoor temperatures by 1.5 to 5 degrees C (2.9 to 9 degrees F). On the other end, street trees — they deliver deep equity, cooling treeless blocks most. In Atlanta, intown neighborhoods can run 5.6 to 8.4 degrees C (10 to 15 degrees F) hotter than its most forested areas. But trees take years to grow shade. So put fast reflectors on hot roofs now, and plant greening where shade inequity runs deepest. Shade sails and misting fill gaps — sails give instant relief along transit routes, misting only one plaza at high water cost. Choose by place, not fashion.

## Slide 13 — Warning Means Nothing Without a Cool Place to Go

_Presenter: Nia Thomas · flow_

Here's the hard truth we've been building toward. We've mapped the heat, we've cooled the surfaces, but each summer, on average, approximately 500 New Yorkers die prematurely because of hot weather, and a warning alone won't change that. So protection has to move like heat moves — block by block. First we sense it, flagging the hottest low-shade blocks with hyperlocal risk maps, because we know risk starts climbing from a median maximum daily temperature of 82°F, long before an official extreme. Second we alert, but not everyone at once. We push heat-health warnings to vulnerable residents first, prioritizing people and communities with the greatest need — because Black New Yorkers had heat-stress death rates three times higher and Latino New Yorkers had death rates two times higher than white New Yorkers. Third we open relief nearby — opening cooling centers and helping eligible neighbors get a free air conditioner with installation through the Home Energy Assistance Program (HEAP) Cooling Assistance Component. And finally we bring care back home, knocking on doors for isolated neighbors, because people who died from heat stress were most often exposed to dangerous heat in homes, and among those exposed at home, none had or were using AC. That's where this chain has to end — cool air where people actually die.

## Slide 14 — Black New Yorkers face 3x the heat death rate

_Presenter: Nia Thomas · chart_

Up until now we've mapped heat and we've cooled pavement and roofs. But here's the hard turn — heat doesn't kill evenly. Black New Yorkers had heat-stress death rates three times higher and Latino New Yorkers had death rates two times higher than white New Yorkers — age-adjusted at 1.2 and 1.0 deaths per million, respectively, compared with 0.4 deaths per million from 2016 to 2025. That's not biology, that's structural racism in housing, energy and care, and it decides who gets to be cool at home. Because home is where people die. People who died from heat stress were most often exposed to dangerous heat in homes, 48%, and among those exposed at home with information on AC, none had or were using it. Atlanta tells the same story from the South: while 96% of residents elsewhere in Atlanta have air conditioning in their homes, less than 80% of Westside residents do — predominantly Black neighborhoods shaped by redlining. So if exposure is at home, protection has to start at home — not at a distant cooling center you can't reach at midnight. That's what this final part is about: getting life-saving cooling, affordable energy, and trusted neighbours to the doorstep before the next heat wave.

## Slide 15 — 0 to 36 months: paint now, protect homes, change code

_Presenter: Nia Thomas · roadmap_

Here's the hard truth: heat doesn't kill on the sidewalk, it kills in the bedroom. On average, approximately 500 New Yorkers die prematurely because of hot weather, and people who died from heat stress were most frequently exposed to heat in homes. Among those exposed at home with information about AC, none had or were using AC. So we stop admiring cool pavements and start this three-year clock to protect people where they sleep. In the first six months we buy time. We paint roofs reflective, which studies show can lower indoor temperatures by 1.5 to 5 degrees C, we open cooling centers prioritizing people and communities with the greatest need, and we add shade and greenery for relief while trees mature. Months six to twelve, we make cooling stick at home. That means a free air conditioner with installation through the Home Energy Assistance Program if you're eligible, no utility shut-offs during hot weather, even if bills are unpaid, and subsidies to help with summer utility costs with affordable clean energy. Then months twelve to thirty-six, we change the rules for good: maximum temperature regulations under Local Law 23 of 2026, expand tree canopy coverage to 30%, and protect the electrical grid by discouraging excess cooling in offices and commercial establishments.
