## Slide 3 — Solid-State Usually Means Hybrid, Not Pure Solid

_Presenter: Leena Shah · definition_

If you cracked open a so-called solid-state cell today, you would probably still find something wet inside. That sounds like a trick, but it's actually good engineering, and it's where our physics story has to start. I call pure solid an ideal, because ions need a continuous path at the atomic scale, not millimeters, to get from anode to cathode and back. Liquids are brilliant at that — they flow, they conform, they self-heal when electrodes swell and shrink. A rigid ceramic can't. It cracks, it leaves voids, it loses touch, especially when you shake it and heat-cycle two materials that expand differently. So what do builders do? They cheat a little, in the best sense. They take an oxide or sulfide ceramic for strength and safety, then paint a thin polymer or gel cushion onto the interface to keep that atomic handshake alive through thousands of cycles. That is what I mean by a hybrid cell — the solid does the heavy lifting, the soft layer saves the contact. Once you see that compromise, you understand why dendrites and durability become our next problems.

<!-- /slide:2 -->

<!-- slide:3 -->
## Slide 4 — Current Flows Only Where Solids Still Touch

_Presenter: Leena Shah · layered-architecture_

Forget chemistry for a moment and think about touch. In the battery you drive today, liquid just flows around everything, it conforms, it self-heals, so even when the cathode swells and shrinks with every charge, ions still find a way across. Take that liquid away and you lose all forgiveness. Now current can flow only where two solids still atomically meet, and you have to hold that meeting through vibration, heat, and thousands of cycles. Look at the stack: your cathode is expanding and contracting, your lithium-metal anode is moving with heat, vibrating, expanding, and between them sits a rigid, brittle electrolyte that cannot flow and cannot heal. The two contacts in between are where we live or die. Lose a few atoms of intimacy there and you get voids, you get cracks, you lose ion paths. Worse, when plating turns uneven at those imperfect contacts, current crowds into hot spots and you seed dendrites. This is why contact loss is so underestimated — it is invisible from outside yet relentless. Nail this physics, and we can talk about dendrites next; fail it, and nothing else about scale or markets matters.

<!-- /slide:3 -->

<!-- slide:4 -->
## Slide 5 — Sulfides Conduct, Oxides Endure

_Presenter: Leena Shah · compare_

Every solid forces you to pick your pain, and this is where the physics gets honest. On one side you have sulfides. Their soft, polarizable lattice lets lithium hop from vacancy to interstitial site with a very low migration barrier, and that open argyrodite framework is essentially a tailored highway for lithium. That's why they move ions so fast. But that same soft chemistry is reactive. It wants to react, so phase purity and mobility come down to synthesis conditions, and you pay for it in moisture control and in pressure and temperature just to keep those pathways open and stable at the interface. On the other side you have oxides — the garnets, perovskites, NASICON-type frameworks. Their rigid atomic framework resists reaction, which is wonderful for chemo-mechanical stability, but rigidity raises activation energies and those polycrystalline layers stack up high grain-boundary resistance that chokes flow. You can fight back with aliovalent doping to create vacancies and with microstructural control and interface engineering, but you still need ultra-flat, pressed contact to keep ions moving. So speed lives with sulfides, endurance lives with oxides. And whichever burden you pick, that interface has to survive atoms shifting over thousands of cycles — which is where we're going next.

<!-- /slide:4 -->

<!-- slide:5 -->
## Slide 6 — Framework Sets the Barrier Every Ion Must Hop

_Presenter: Leena Shah · flow_

Forget the factory for a minute and shrink down to the atomic scale, because that's where a solid-state cell is won or lost. In a liquid, lithium just flows. In a solid, it can't. It has to hop — from vacancy to vacancy, slipping through interstitial sites in the crystal lattice. No vacancy, no hop. So the chemistry trick is deliberate imperfection: aliovalent doping to generate vacancies, more defects meaning more paths. And the framework you choose sets the height of every barrier. Sulfides, especially those open argyrodite structures, are soft and polarizable. The lattice breathes a little around the lithium, lowering the migration barrier, which is why they give us the highest bulk conductivity of the families. Oxides are the opposite bargain. Your garnets and perovskites, NASICON-types too, are rigid and stable — they endure. But in a polycrystalline ceramic, every grain boundary is a toll booth. Depending on microstructure and interface chemistry, it can help or, far more often in oxides, add real resistance at each crossing. That trade-off — fast but soft versus stable but blocked — is our physics baseline. Once you see it, you can understand why contact loss and dendrites hurt so much, which is where we're going next.

## Slide 8 — Four atomic flaws decide if millions of cells survive

_Presenter: Omar Khan · matrix_

One perfect cell in a glovebox doesn't make a car go. That's the turn we're making now — from physics to production.

In the lab we can baby a cell, keep it flat, press it, cycle it gently. At millions of cells per month, you get variability, vibration, thermal gradients, fast charging and years of abuse. And a liquid would just flow, conform and self-heal. A solid can't.

That's why these four atomic flaws decide everything. Top right are dendrites — our dominant failure mode. Lithium gets impatient at hot spots, plates unevenly, then runs along grain boundaries to the cathode. Once it bridges, you short, heat locally, and risk thermal runaway. Even before that it steals lithium as dead, isolated metal.

Cracks are the brittle partner — low KIc ceramics fail in tension, and inherent flaws grow to fracture. Bottom half is slower death. Voids from expansion and vibration open gaps where ions simply stop, so capacity fades. And heat cycling pries joints apart because two solids with different coefficients of thermal expansion never quite move together over thousands of cycles.

So rare at lab scale still means catastrophic at gigafactory scale. That's the yield problem we'll unpack next.

<!-- /slide:7 -->

<!-- slide:8 -->
## Slide 9 — Uneven Plating Splits Into Three Failures

_Presenter: Omar Khan · branching-flow_

Here's the moment beautiful chemistry turns ugly in the real world. We've talked physics, how lithium has to plate back onto the anode every time you charge. And lithium is impatient. If current density is uneven, if contact is imperfect, if interfaces contain defects such as grain boundaries, it doesn't wait for a perfect flat layer. It piles up at microscopic hot spots, and those hot spots grow into needle-like metallic structures we call dendrites. In a liquid cell that's bad. In a solid cell it's fatal, because those dendrites propagate right along the grain boundaries of the electrolyte itself. And from there I see three different ways we lose. First, if that metal filament reaches the cathode, the cell shorts internally, local heating accelerates, and thermal runaway becomes likely. Second, even without a short, it steals lithium by creating electrically isolated metal, what we call dead lithium, reducing the amount of active material available for future cycles. And third, and this is what keeps manufacturing people up at night, growth is stochastic and happens deep inside sealed cells, far from direct observation. Small variations in defects, contact quality, and local current density mean you can't predict when it fails. So as we move from perfect lab cells to millions per month, this variability is our scaling problem.

## Slide 10 — Lab Comfort Fails Gigafactory Reality

_Presenter: Omar Khan · before-after_

Here's the uncomfortable truth we've been circling: what works in a glovebox doesn't survive a Gigafactory. In the lab we've learned how to cheat physics just enough to get beautiful data. We hand-build perfect little cells, we cycle them gently, and we clamp them under stack pressure to force those rigid solids to hold that uninterrupted atomic-scale contact. Take the clamp away and contact breaks, resistance spikes, dendrites find a crack. Now put that in a factory. The battery industry isn't judged on chemistry, it's judged on yield, cost, throughput and defect tolerance. You have to ship millions of cells per month that tolerate variability, vibration, thermal gradients, fast charging and years of abuse, with no press to pamper them. And solid-state makes that harder, not easier. You need ultra-flat interfaces, high stack pressures, brittle ceramic layers, and extreme moisture control. Each one is a brand new failure risk liquid cells never had. So a defect rate that's a rounding error in the lab becomes catastrophic at scale. Performance isn't our blocker anymore. Scale is. And that's what this next section is about: why building one good cell is science, but building millions is a completely different war.

## Slide 11 — Every solid electrolyte trades yield for performance

_Presenter: Omar Khan · scorecard_

Physics doesn't pay the bills — yield does. We've just seen why ions can move through a solid; now the question is whether we can make that happen a million times a month without defects killing us. Every family makes a different bargain, and that's why I score them this way. Yield tolerance gets the biggest weight, 30%, because scale is not performance, it's defect tolerance, throughput and cost. Sulfides conduct well but they demand dry rooms and extreme moisture control. Any slip and you lose the interface. Oxides are very stable, but they're brittle ceramics — they demand perfection. Ultra-flat interfaces, high stack pressures, and that high grain boundary resistance we talked about means one crack or void breaks atomic-scale contact for good. Polymer hybrids are easier to process, but less stable — pragmatic, not transformative. And scale punishes all three, because only around 40 percent of today's machines and processes can be used for producing solid-state batteries, and even a small pilot plant in the megawatt range is expected to cost between 500 million and one billion euros. Add cells that breathe — up to ten centimeters on the battery system level — inside a rigid frame, and you see why lab success is not manufacturing readiness.

## Slide 13 — Low Reuse Forces a Premium-First Entry Path

_Presenter: Priya Nair · chart_

Here's the hard math that decides who actually gets solid-state first. We've spent the last two sections on why the physics is difficult and why the factory is even harder. Now that hits your wallet. Only around 40 percent of today's machines and processes can be used for producing solid-state batteries. That means you can't just retrofit a gigafactory — you have to rebuild most of it from scratch. And rebuilding is brutally expensive. The development and construction alone for a small pilot plant in the megawatt range is expected to cost between 500 million and one billion euros. That's before you've made a single sellable cell at volume. Add the geopolitical reality: production of LIBs is currently dominated by Chinese cell manufacturers, who supply more than 50 percent of the global LIB market. So you're trying to break in against an incumbent that already owns scale, supply chains, and cost. So what? You don't start with a family sedan. You start where performance takes precedence over cost — racing or aviation — then move into high-priced vehicle segments once you prove you outrun lithium-ion, and only then chase disruption for everyone else. If you try to go mass-market on day one, the economics will kill you.

## Slide 14 — Race First, Premium Next, Everyone After

_Presenter: Priya Nair · roadmap_

Here's the hard truth about new battery tech: it never starts where we want it to. It starts where someone can afford to pay for performance. After everything we've covered on atomic-scale contact and why scale is so brutal — where only around 40 percent of today's machines and processes can be used — you can see why solid-state won't debut in a family hatchback. So phase one is performance over cost. Think racing, where winning matters more than the price tag. Think aviation, where power matters more than price. That's why those first eVTOLs — electrically powered aircraft similar to helicopters — are such perfect test beds. They need the energy density and will tolerate the cost. Then, once performance outpaces the competition and manufacturing learns a little, we move to phase two: high-priced vehicle segments. That's the promise we've been chasing: premium long range, up to 800 kilometers on a single charge, with fast acceleration and quick charging. Only then comes phase three, disruption. Significant cost and performance improvements finally enable widespread introduction for all applications — not just cars, but trucks and buses too. And if we get that breakthrough, it doesn't just tweak the drivetrain portfolio for passenger cars. It rewrites it.

## Slide 15 — Win Premium Now to Earn the Mass Market Next

_Presenter: Priya Nair · pros-cons_

Here’s the trap: if we aim solid-state straight at the affordable family car, we fail. We’ve just seen why scale punishes us, so we have to earn scale instead. The winning sequence is performance first, cost second. Start where performance takes precedence over cost — in racing, where power matters more than price, and in aviation, where our eVTOL demonstrators — those electrically powered aircraft similar to helicopters — have to prove safety under real abuse. That credibility buys us the second step: high-priced vehicle segments, premium EVs where buyers will actually pay extra for up to 800 kilometers on a single charge, and more than 800 kilometers at the same, or even lower, weight. That early revenue is not vanity, it pays for the learning — the dry rooms, the move to press-free yields that let us escape the lab. But let’s be honest about what still blocks disruption, the third phase for trucks and buses and mass EVs. Mass means producing millions of cells per month, without babysitting each one. Sulfides demand extreme moisture control, oxides demand flawless pressing. Only around 40 percent of today’s machines and processes can be reused, a pilot alone costs between 500 million and one billion euros, and China supplies more than 50 percent of the global LIB market until those new lines scale.

Don't take my word for any of this — that's the point.
