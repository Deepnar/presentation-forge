## Slide 4 — Seven Seconds, Then the Picture Hardens

## Slide 5 — Verbal Content Carries Under 35 Percent of the Meaning

_Presenter: Rudrapratap Singh · bullets_

Think about the last time someone told you, I'm totally fine, while their arms were crossed, their voice was flat, and they wouldn't look at you. Who did you believe — the sentence or the body? You believed the body, and so does everyone you meet. We've just talked about that seven-second window where people decide who you are. This is why that window is so unforgiving. In a typical interaction, verbal content carries less than 35% of the total social meaning. The rest is communicated through tone, gesture, posture, and silence. And when those two channels contradict each other, when a student receives two contradictory messages — one verbal and one non-verbal — they are more likely to believe the non-verbal one, because that is where they think your true intention is leaking out. I see this on campus all the time: I am happy to answer questions, said while rushing, closed off, checking a phone. No one asks. The fastest way to lose the room is with your voice. Research has shown that low amounts of vocal variation have the most negatively impactful results on a teacher's perceived credibility, and the same is true for us. A monotone kills you faster than a wrong answer. So rehearse the nonverbal line before you rehearse the verbal one. Next, we'll break that line down piece by piece, starting with what your face and posture are saying before you speak.

## Slide 7 — Four Levers Beyond Words Decide How Students Are Read

_Presenter: Sakshi Singh · feature-grid_

Think of walking into a seminar room and deciding who looks approachable before anyone says hello. That is where we are moving now — from how fast we judge to what we are actually judging. Research suggests verbal content carries less than 35% of the total social meaning in a typical interaction, so the rest is you before your words. First, your eyes. In many Western societies, direct eye contact is associated with honesty and engagement, while in some Asian cultures, prolonged eye contact may be considered disrespectful or confrontational, so confidence is not staring harder, it is calibrating. They can emphasize points and convey enthusiasm, but when they never stop, we read nerves, not passion. And finally, your voice. ## Slide 9 — Low-Context Cultures Say It; High-Context Cultures Show It

_Presenter: Udaypratap Singh · bullets_

Think about the last time you thought you were being clear, and someone else thought you were being rude. That gap is where I want to start our new section on culture and context. Until now we have talked about body language like it means the same thing to everyone. Edward T. Hall told us back in 1959 in The Silent Language that culture is communication and communication is culture. In low-context cultures meaning lives in explicit verbal messages, in words spoken plainly. In high-context cultures intention is carried through implicit contexts, through gestures, social customs, silence, nuance, or tone of voice. So what feels honest to you can feel harsh to someone else. Low-context is direct and confrontational, politeness is almost secondary and identity is based on the individual. High-context is indirect and polite, politeness is essential because identity is based on the social system. You see it on campus every day: direct eye contact we praise in a U.S. classroom as honesty becomes disrespect toward a superior in Hispanic, Asian, Middle Eastern, and Native American cultures. A head nod up and down is not a universal yes, gestures travel badly — President George H. Bush's V for Victory salute read as an insult in Australia, and President Nixon's OK circle flashed exiting an airplane in Brazil landed the same way. If we do not read context, we misread people.

## Slide 10 — Hofstede's Six Dimensions Map How American Students Read the Room

_Presenter: Vicky Singh · bullets_

Here's the trap — you can do everything right, firm handshake, steady eye contact, open posture, and still completely misread the person in front of you. Because until now we've talked about body language like it's universal, and it's not. Culture is the decoder, and that's where Geert Hofstede gives us a map. Think of how we as American students walk into a room. We rank number one in the world on individualism, so we hear a self-introduction as personal achievement, sell yourself. We sit 16th from the bottom on power distance, so we expect informal, first-name, egalitarian chat, where someone from Malaysia or Guatemala might expect deference to rank. We rank 64th out of 76 on uncertainty avoidance, so ambiguity feels fun to us, while a classmate from Greece or Portugal can find open-ended small talk genuinely stressful. We're 19th out of 76 on masculinity, so we blend assertiveness with relational warmth. We're 31st out of 39 toward short-term orientation, which means tradition and saving face in the moment matter more than distant payoff. And we rank 15th alongside Canada and the Netherlands on indulgence, so expressive, friendly networking feels natural. If you don't know your default, you'll mistake someone else's respect for distance. And that's exactly why next we have to talk about face and blind spots.

## Slide 13 — A Five-Step Loop From First Contact to Lasting Check-In

_Presenter: Divyajyot Sinha · flow_

Think about the last opportunity you heard about — internship, fest team, research assistant gig. Chances are it didn't come from a portal, it came from someone who knew someone. ## Slide 15 — Introductions Improve With Rehearsal, Not Nerve

_Presenter: Deepesh Sonar · checklist_

Here's what nobody tells you before your first career fair — confidence isn't what makes you good at introductions, rehearsal does. ## Slide 19 — Cultural Intelligence Is Four Learnable Levers, Not a Gift

_Presenter: Dhwani Tandon · bullets_

Here’s the turn we’ve been waiting for — we’ve named our biases, we’ve felt how fragile face can be, so what do you actually do differently in your next hello across cultures? In 2003, P. Christopher Earley and Soon Ang gave us a practical answer with cultural intelligence, your capability to function effectively in situations characterized by cultural diversity. And here’s why I love it for us as students: it isn’t talent, it’s four levers you can build. First, cognitive CQ is knowledge. Learn how a gesture lives elsewhere — back home we’re told to look someone in the eye, yet in Hispanic, Asian, Middle Eastern, and Native American cultures direct contact with someone superior can read as disrespect. Second, motivational CQ is desire. You can know everything and still stay on the sidelines if you don’t actually want to engage. Third, metacognitive CQ is awareness in the moment, checking whether your assumption fits the person in front of you, not just their group. Fourth, behavioral CQ is action, adapting your words and nonverbals to bridge the gap, never expecting them to adjust to you. Most of us will score between 12 and 18 on each factor at first — deliberate practice moves it. And what glues it together is mindful interaction: attention, intention, and an open, kind, curious attitude.
